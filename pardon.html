Presidential Pardon Application · Pending

I asked the President for a pardon.

More than a decade ago, in the depths of an opioid addiction that began with painkillers prescribed for a military injury, I sold my closest friend, Justin, a gram of heroin — at the same price I'd paid for it. He died. I was charged federally, accepted responsibility from the first day, and served a five-year sentence. Today I study the drug supply at UCLA — and my presidential pardon application is pending with the Office of the Pardon Attorney.

What happened

Justin and I were best friends, and we were both addicted to heroin. In 2014 I sold him a gram — at cost, the same price I'd paid, the same as we always did — and he died. Federal prosecutors charged me with conspiracy to distribute heroin. There was no commercial trafficking, no profit, no organization; there was addiction, and there was grief. I said so from the first day, pleaded guilty, and served my five years.

I got to recovery in March 2015 — in jail, not because of it. I served my federal time at FCI Dublin. The week I left custody, I enrolled at Portland State University; three years later I graduated summa cum laude. A federal judge terminated my supervision early, "in the interests of justice." Since then I've published peer-reviewed research in JAMA, founded the overdose-prevention nonprofit Beats Overdose, helped shape Oregon drug policy, and joined UCLA's Drug Checking Los Angeles, where my research on the illicit fentanyl supply has been covered by The New York Times, NPR, and CNN.

A pardon would not erase what happened. Nothing does. It would recognize what I've done with every day since — and let me carry this work everywhere it's needed.

Why a pardon

The sentence ended years ago. The conviction didn't. A decade of recovery, degrees, and peer-reviewed science later, it still follows me into rooms it has no business being in — and for a federal conviction, a presidential pardon is the only remedy that exists.

  • My research careerEvery background check reopens 2014Hiring at both UCLA and Northeastern required extra review after HR flagged the conviction — senior faculty had to advocate internally for me. As recently as May 2026, my own university's public-safety office flagged it again and required a written explanation before clearing me. Twelve years later, as a graduate student, I am still explaining.
  • The scienceI can't present my research where it's discussedMy work on the fentanyl supply is read internationally, but I often can't present it in person: the UK refused me in 2022 and again in 2025 under a mandatory no-appeal rule, and Australia refused a visitor visa in June 2026. I've missed conferences where my own research was on the program, and it hampers my collaboration with the EU Drugs Agency.
  • No other doorThere is no federal expungementFor a federal conviction there is no expungement, no sealing, no set-aside. Congress has created no mechanism at all. A presidential pardon is the only relief that exists — which is exactly what it's for.
  • What it would sayOfficial recognition that recovery is realI've been in recovery since March 2015. I work, publish, teach, and pay taxes. A pardon is the country saying, on the record, that people can come all the way back — a message for everyone in recovery and reentry, not just me. And it lets me keep honoring Justin with work that saves lives.

Questions, answered

What is Morgan seeking a pardon for?
A full presidential pardon of her 2015 federal conviction for conspiracy to distribute heroin (U.S. v. Godvin, No. 3:14-cr-00267-BR, D. Or.) — a conviction stemming from selling a gram of heroin, at the price she'd paid for it, to a friend during her own addiction. Not commercial trafficking. She completed her sentence in full.
What's the current status?
Pending before the Office of the Pardon Attorney as clemency case No. P314982 ("pardon after completion of sentence"). Applied 2024, docketed 2025, supplemental memorandum filed May 2026. Anyone can verify the status in the DOJ clemency database.
Why does a pardon matter if the sentence is long over?
Three reasons. There is no federal expungement — a pardon is the only remedy that exists for a federal conviction. The conviction still surfaces on every background check, twelve years on: UCLA, Northeastern, and (in May 2026) her own university have all flagged it, requiring intervention or written explanation. And it keeps her from presenting her research at the international conferences where it's discussed — mandatory UK refusals in 2022 and 2025, and an Australian visitor-visa refusal in June 2026.
Who decides, and how does the process work?
The Office of the Pardon Attorney at the Department of Justice reviews petitions and makes recommendations; the pardon power itself belongs solely to the President under Article II of the Constitution. There is no deadline — petitions can be pending for years.
