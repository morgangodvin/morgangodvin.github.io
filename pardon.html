Presidential Pardon Application · Pending

I asked the President for a pardon.

More than a decade ago, in the depths of an opioid addiction that began with painkillers prescribed for a military injury, I shared a gram of heroin with my closest friend, Justin. He died. I was charged federally, accepted responsibility from the first day, and served a five-year sentence. Today I study the drug supply at UCLA — and my presidential pardon application is pending with the Office of the Pardon Attorney.

What happened

Justin and I were best friends, and we were both addicted to heroin. In 2014 I shared a gram with him — the same as we always did — and he died. Federal prosecutors charged me with conspiracy to distribute heroin, part of an enforcement strategy of that era that charged drug users as traffickers after an overdose death. There was no commercial trafficking, no profit, no organization; there was addiction, and there was grief. I said so from the first day, pleaded guilty, and served my five years.

I got to recovery in March 2015 — in jail, not because of it. I served my federal time at FCI Dublin. The week I left custody, I enrolled at Portland State University; three years later I graduated summa cum laude. A federal judge terminated my supervision early, "in the interests of justice." Since then I've published peer-reviewed research in JAMA, founded the overdose-prevention nonprofit Beats Overdose, helped shape Oregon drug policy, and joined UCLA's Drug Checking Los Angeles, where my research on the illicit fentanyl supply has been covered by The New York Times, NPR, and CNN.

A pardon would not erase what happened. Nothing does. It would recognize what I've done with every day since — and let me carry this work everywhere it's needed.

The U.S. Attorney who oversaw my prosecution supports the pardon.

S. Amanda Marshall served as United States Attorney for the District of Oregon from 2011 to 2015, the office that prosecuted my case. She wrote to the Office of the Pardon Attorney in support of my petition:

"Ms. Godvin's federal conviction is not related in any way to commercial drug trafficking… the offence was based on her personal use within a social circle of other drug-users, not commercial distribution, profit-seeking, or organized criminal activity."

What the conviction still costs

Overdose is a global crisis, and drug-supply research is global work. A decade after the sentence ended, the conviction still closes borders — and only a presidential pardon opens them.

  • United Kingdom · 2022 & 2025Visa and travel authorization refused — mandatory, no appealUK rules bar anyone with a custodial sentence of 12 months or more, with "no right of appeal or right to administrative review." I've missed multiple academic conferences as a result.
  • Australia · June 2, 2026Visitor visa refused — no right of merits reviewRefused a month ago. Earlier, a fully funded PhD offer at the University of New South Wales was impossible to accept: the university's own immigration counsel advised I had essentially no hope of a student visa.
  • PortugalImmigration counsel: application would be rejectedPortugal is home to the EU Drugs Agency, where I've been an invited speaker and study visitor — work I can do on short stays but never build on.

Questions, answered

What is Morgan seeking a pardon for?
A full presidential pardon of her 2015 federal conviction for conspiracy to distribute heroin (U.S. v. Godvin, No. 3:14-cr-00267-BR, D. Or.) — a conviction stemming from sharing heroin with a friend during her own addiction, not commercial trafficking. She completed her sentence in full.
What's the current status?
Pending before the Office of the Pardon Attorney as clemency case No. P314982 ("pardon after completion of sentence"). Applied 2024, docketed 2025, supplemental memorandum filed May 2026. Anyone can verify the status in the DOJ clemency database.
Why does a pardon matter if the sentence is long over?
Because the conviction operates as a permanent international travel ban for a researcher whose field is global: mandatory UK refusals in 2022 and 2025, an Australian refusal in June 2026 (plus a fully funded PhD offer she couldn't accept), and a Portuguese application counsel advised would be rejected. A pardon is the only remedy for these barriers.
Who decides, and how does the process work?
The Office of the Pardon Attorney at the Department of Justice reviews petitions and makes recommendations; the pardon power itself belongs solely to the President under Article II of the Constitution. There is no deadline — petitions can be pending for years.
